Part 1

Introduction to court reporting

Why the press gallery matters to access to justice.

Open justice
“Justice should not only be done, but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done.”Lord Hewart CJ, R v Sussex Justices (1924)

The reporter is the public’s eyes in court

  • Accountability — judges, prosecutors, police and lawyers act in public view.
  • Access to justice — exposing delays, costs, detention and unequal treatment.
  • Public understanding — explaining rights, procedure and the law.
  • Deterrence & confidence — a visible system earns trust.
Two kinds of output

From “what happened today” to “why it matters”

📰 Court report

  • One case, one sitting
  • Charges, evidence, rulings, next date
  • Strictly what was said in open court
  • Fast, frequent, factual

⚖️ Justice story

  • Patterns across many cases
  • Systems: delays, detention, legal aid, cost
  • Data, documents, voices outside court
  • Asks: who is failed, and why?

Part 2

Story identification & news values

Seeing the story behind the cause list.

News values through a justice lens

What makes a court matter newsworthy?

Impact

How many people does the ruling or failure affect?

Prominence

Public officials, institutions, well-known parties.

Precedent

A first-of-its-kind ruling or new interpretation of law.

Conflict

State vs citizen, rights vs power, community vs company.

Human interest

Ordinary lives changed by the justice system.

Proximity

Local courts, local defendants, local consequences.

Timeliness

Verdicts, sentencing, bail, deadlines, anniversaries.

Pattern

The tenth adjournment is a story; so is the hundredth detainee.

Look beyond the verdict

Where justice stories hide

⏳ Delay

Repeated adjournments, absent prosecutors, missing case files, “no power today”.

🔒 Detention

Awaiting-trial detainees held longer than the maximum sentence for the offence.

💸 Bail & cost

Bail terms poor defendants can never meet; filing fees and hidden charges.

🧑‍⚖️ Representation

Defendants without lawyers; overstretched legal aid; no interpreter.

📄 Compliance

Court orders and judgments ignored by government or powerful parties.

🏛 Infrastructure

Courtrooms without records, recording, ramps or space for the public.

Activity · vote, then reveal

Find the strongest angle

A magistrate adjourns a petty-theft case for the 14th time because the prosecution witness is absent again. The defendant, accused of stealing a phone, has been in custody for three years.
Best: B. The court date is just the peg; the public-interest story is pre-trial detention and delay. Check how many other cases on the cause list share the pattern, and seek comment from the prosecution, the defence, the court registry and the custody authority.
A High Court orders a state agency to pay compensation to 200 families evicted from a waterfront settlement. Eighteen months later, no payment has been made and families are still in temporary shelters.
Best: C. Non-compliance with court orders is a core access-to-justice story. Get the certified judgment, document the timeline, give the agency a right of reply, and use human voices (B) inside this story rather than instead of it.
A quick filter

In the public interest — or just interesting to the public?

?

Does it affect many people, or the vulnerable?

Rights, safety, liberty, money, services.

?

Does it expose wrongdoing or system failure?

Abuse of power, corruption, negligence, unlawful detention.

?

Does it help people make decisions?

Knowing their rights, where to seek help, how the law applies.

?

Does it hold power to account?

Public bodies, officials, institutions, corporations.

Yes to one or more → you likely have a public-interest story.

Part 3

Observation, note-taking & verification

Accuracy is won or lost in the courtroom notebook.

Courtroom observation

Before you enter & what to watch

🗂 Before

  • Check the cause list; get the case number and court
  • Obtain the charge sheet or originating process
  • Read reports of earlier sittings
  • Know the parties, counsel and judge
  • Learn the court’s rules on phones, recording and photos

👁 During

  • Who is present — and who is absent
  • Charges read and pleas taken
  • Key testimony, in the witness’s exact words
  • Exhibits tendered; objections and rulings
  • Adjournment: new date and the reason
  • What didn’t happen that should have

Part 4

Writing & ethical reporting

Fair, accurate and contemporaneous — and do no harm.

Presumption of innocence · tap to flip

Responsible language: an accused is not a convict

Reporting allegations without prejudicing a trial

Report the trial — don’t conduct it

✅ Safe ground

  • What was said and done in open court
  • Fair, accurate, balanced and timely
  • Every allegation attributed (“the prosecution alleged…”)
  • The defence case reported alongside the prosecution’s

⛔ Risky ground

  • Comment on guilt or innocence before verdict
  • Previous convictions or “evidence” not before the court
  • Material the court has excluded or ordered not published
  • Interviewing witnesses about their evidence mid-trial
  • “Trial by media” campaigns, inflammatory photos and captions
Activity · fair or prejudicial?

Test the headline

Corrupt ex-minister finally faces justice
Prejudicial.“Corrupt” presumes guilt. Try: “Ex-minister goes on trial over alleged contract fraud.”
Witness tells court she did not see accused take the money
Fair.Accurate, attributed report of testimony given in open court.
Pastor accused of abuse has done it before, neighbours say
Prejudicial.Out-of-court claims of past conduct, not before the court, could influence the trial.
Three years without trial: detainee’s case adjourned for 14th time
Fair.A factual, public-interest angle on delay that makes no claim about guilt.
Killer driver smiles as charges are read
Prejudicial.“Killer” presumes guilt; demeanour commentary invites judgement. Report the charge and plea.

Interviewing relevant stakeholders ethically

Who to talk to — and how

👥 Who

  • Prosecution and defence counsel
  • Defendants — through their lawyers
  • Families and affected communities
  • Court registry and public relations officers
  • Legal aid bodies, civil society, academics

🤝 How

  • Informed consent — explain use, platform and risks
  • Don’t discuss evidence with witnesses during trial
  • Trauma-informed — patience, privacy, the right to stop
  • No payment or promises in exchange for information
  • Right of reply for anyone criticised

Weak intro

“The long-awaited trial of the notorious fraud case continued on Tuesday at the High Court amid tension.”

Strong intro

“A cooperative treasurer told the High Court on Tuesday that members’ savings had vanished from their account, but admitted she never saw the accused trader withdraw the money.”

One case, three formats

Choosing the right format

Court report

What happened today

300–500 words. Strict chronology of proceedings, attribution in every paragraph, filed same day.

News story

What changed

Rulings, verdicts, sentencing, bail. Adds reaction, context and what happens next.

Human-interest feature

What it means

Lives behind the case. Narrative, scenes, voices — still verified, still fair, still careful about live proceedings.

Ethical reporting involving children and vulnerable persons

Protect identity — minimise harm

  • Never identify children involved in proceedings — as defendants, witnesses or complainants
  • Protect survivors of sexual and gender-based violence
  • Take care with people with mental-health conditions or disabilities
  • Blur faces, change voices, avoid school uniforms and landmarks
  • Ask: does this detail serve the public, or only curiosity?

Part 5

Practical court-reporting exercise

From notebook to publishable report.

Your reporter’s notes · fictional case

The State v. K. Adeyemi

FICTIONAL · TRAINING ONLY

Court: High Court 3, Courtroom 2 · Tuesday. Sitting began 2 hours late; the judge said there had been a power outage.

Charges: Three counts — conspiracy, obtaining by false pretences and stealing 48 million in savings belonging to the Unity Market Women’s Cooperative. Pleaded not guilty in March.

Custody: In custody since November last year; bail application refused in January.

Testimony: Second prosecution witness, Mrs R. Eze, 56, cooperative treasurer: “We gave him our contributions every Friday. In January, the account was empty.”

Cross-examination (defence counsel B. Okafor): she agreed she never personally saw the defendant withdraw money.

Exhibit P4: bank statement of the cooperative account, admitted without objection.

Gallery: About 40 cooperative members attended; some wept. One shouted “Thief!” and was warned by the judge.

Outside court: Mr Okafor told reporters: “My client is a scapegoat.”

Next witness: A 15-year-old girl, daughter of a member. Her mother gave you the girl’s name and school.

Adjourned to 9 December for continuation of trial.

“Thief!”

An outburst, not evidence. Don’t headline it.

“Scapegoat”

Outside court — attribute it clearly and keep it in proportion.

The child

No name, school or detail that identifies her.

The delay

Power outage + months in custody = a follow-up justice story.

Your task

Write a 150–200 word court report

  • Strong intro: who, what charge, what happened today
  • Attribute every allegation
  • Include the defence’s position
  • End with the next date
  • Then swap with a partner and peer-review

Part 6

Questions & recap

Rapid recap

Three quick questions

1. Before a verdict, the person giving evidence of a crime against them is best called…

“Complainant” (or “alleged victim”) avoids presuming the crime happened.

2. A note marked [OBS] in your notebook is…

Keep observations separate — never present them as testimony.

3. Naming a child witness’s school is…

Combined details can identify a child as surely as a name.
Take these with you

Six principles of justice reporting

1

Be present

The public relies on you to see what happens in court.

2

Look for patterns

Delay, detention and cost are stories, not background.

3

Note precisely

Exact words, times — and facts kept apart from impressions.

4

Presume innocence

Attribute every allegation. Only the court decides guilt.

5

Verify everything

Documents, registry, second sources, right of reply.

6

Do no harm

Protect children, survivors and the vulnerable.
